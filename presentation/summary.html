Paper summary

Gender Bias in LLM Hiring Decisions — Evidence from Japan

Do large language models favor a candidate's gender when screening résumés in a Japanese hiring context — and can two practical fixes stop it?

arXiv:2606.18649 Counterfactual résumé design 5 LLMs · 43,200 evaluations
Purpose

Testing Western bias findings in a non-Western setting

Prior work found that modern LLMs tend to favor female applicants — but almost entirely with English résumés and American hiring. This paper asks whether the same pattern holds in a Japanese corporate context, and whether prompt instructions or name redaction can reduce it.

Research questions

Four questions

1

Do LLMs show gender bias when evaluating Japanese job candidates?

2

Does the industry's gender makeup (Finance vs. Healthcare) affect the bias?

3

Can a prompt telling the model to ignore gender reduce the bias?

4

Can removing candidate names (anonymization) reduce the bias?

Methodology

Change only the name — hold everything else constant

A controlled counterfactual design: each résumé was scored many times with only the applicant's name swapped, so any score difference is attributable to perceived gender alone.

Evaluations

Dataset

60 résumés converted into traditional Japanese rirekisho format, spanning two industries and four roles:

  • Finance — finance manager, secretary
  • Healthcare — doctor, nurse

Models tested

  • Claude Sonnet 4.6
  • GPT-4o
  • Gemini 2.5 Flash
  • DeepSeek V3
  • Llama 3.3 70B

Evaluation criteria

Each model scored candidates 1–10 on four dimensions: role relevance, professional experience, Japanese workplace cultural fit, and overall evaluation.

Key findings

What the experiment showed

1 · Every model favored female candidates

Across all résumés, female-named applicants consistently scored higher than identical male-named applicants. The effect was modest but highly consistent, and stayed statistically significant after controlling for résumé, model, and name pair.

2 · GPT-4o showed the strongest bias

3 · Industry barely mattered

Finance (male-dominated) and Healthcare (female-dominated) produced nearly identical pro-female bias. Even male-dominated roles like finance manager and doctor favored female-named applicants.

4 · Prompt engineering did not fix it

The fairness prompt
Ignore the candidate's name and gender. Evaluate only skills and experience.

This had almost no effect — bias stayed significant for all five models. Simply instructing a model to "be fair" is ineffective.

5 · Removing names almost eliminated the bias

The paper's most important result. Instead of asking the model to ignore names, the researchers deleted them before evaluation:

Haruto Sato → [PRIVATE_PERSON]
Central takeaway

With names removed, gender bias nearly disappeared and male and female résumés scored essentially the same. The candidate's name is the primary channel through which LLMs infer gender.

Unexpected finding

The redaction catch

42%
When names became [PRIVATE_PERSON], GPT-4o refused 42% of requests — its safety system flagged the redacted text. Anonymization works in theory, but some models need careful implementation.
Why it matters

The pattern crosses cultures

Earlier studies found pro-female bias with English résumés, American hiring, and Western companies. This paper shows the same pattern in Japanese-language prompts, Japanese résumé formats, and Japanese corporate scenarios — suggesting the bias stems from LLM alignment methods rather than Western culture alone.

Limitations

What to keep in mind

  • Japanese résumés were generated from American résumés, not written by native Japanese professionals.
  • Only one fairness prompt was tested.
  • GPT-4o privacy-filter results were incomplete due to refusals.
  • Only five models were evaluated.
  • Each résumé was scored once at temperature 0, so output variability wasn't measured.
Final conclusion

What the study concludes

  • ♀Modern LLMs gave higher hiring scores to female-named applicants, even on identical résumés.
  • 🌐The bias generalized from Western settings to a Japanese hiring context.
  • ✗Telling models to ignore gender does not meaningfully reduce bias.
  • ✓Removing names before evaluation is far more effective — names are the primary source of gender inference.
  • ⚙Organizations should favor system-level anonymization over prompt-based fairness instructions.
